Light arrives in packets

Shine light on a clean metal and electrons can be knocked out of it. Around 1900 this looked simple, but the details did not fit the idea of light as a smooth wave. Einstein explained them in 1905 by treating light as a stream of packets, photons, each carrying an energy set only by its colour: E = h f. It earned him the 1921 Nobel Prize.

Three things a wave cannot explain

A threshold. Below a certain frequency no electron leaves, however bright the light. One photon must bring at least the work function W, the energy that binds an electron to the metal. Photons do not team up.

Colour sets the energy. Above the threshold the fastest electrons leave with Kmax = h f − W. Bluer light gives faster electrons. Brighter light does not.

Brightness sets the number. More intense light means more photons per second, so more electrons and a larger current, each with the same energy as before.

Try it

Pick zinc and slide the colour from red to ultraviolet: nothing happens until about 288 nm. Then make the voltage negative. The collector now repels the electrons, and the current falls to zero at the stopping voltage, which in volts equals Kmax in electron-volts. This is how Kmax is measured.

The graph

Kmax against frequency is a straight line for every metal. The lines are parallel: their slope is Planck's constant h. Where a line meets the axis is that metal's threshold frequency.

About the model

Work functions: cesium 2.1 eV, sodium 2.3 eV, zinc 4.3 eV, copper 4.7 eV (typical values; real surfaces vary). Every photon above the threshold frees one electron here, while in a real tube only a small fraction do. Electrons leave with a spread of energies and directions up to Kmax and move in a uniform field between the plates. Ultraviolet light is invisible; it is drawn violet.
