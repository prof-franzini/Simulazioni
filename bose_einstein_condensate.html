A new state of matter

Cool a dilute gas of bosons to a few hundred billionths of a degree above absolute zero and something abrupt happens: a large share of the atoms drops into the single lowest quantum state of the trap. They stop behaving as separate particles and act as one matter wave. Bose and Einstein predicted it in 1924–25; it was first made in 1995 with rubidium and sodium (Nobel Prize 2001).

Why it happens

Every atom is also a wave, with a wavelength that grows as the atom slows down (λ = h / √(2π m k T)). In a warm gas the waves are much shorter than the distance between atoms. Cooling stretches them until neighbours overlap. For bosons, which are allowed to share one state, overlap triggers condensation: it starts when the phase-space density nλ³ reaches 2.61.

What you see

Orange dots are thermal atoms, each moving on its own. Blue is the condensate: dense, still and coherent. The surface below is what experiments record: a shadow picture of the cloud, drawn as height and colour. A broad hill is thermal gas; the sharp spike that rises through it below the critical temperature is the condensate.

Release trap

Switch the trap off and the cloud flies apart. The thermal gas spreads equally in all directions. The condensate does not: it expands fastest along the direction in which it was squeezed hardest, so a flat disc turns into a tall cigar. This inversion was the first proof of condensation.

Stir

A condensate is a superfluid and cannot rotate like water in a cup. It can only carry rotation in quantised vortices, thin holes that arrange themselves in a regular triangular lattice.

About the model

500 000 atoms of rubidium-87 in a harmonic trap (80 Hz across, 200 Hz vertically). The condensed share follows the ideal-gas law N₀/N = 1 − (T/Tc)³, the condensate has the Thomas–Fermi shape set by repulsion between atoms, and its expansion follows the Castin–Dum scaling equations. Each dot stands for about 60 atoms, time is slowed about 400 times and vortex cores are drawn larger than they are.
