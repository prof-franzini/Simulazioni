Where is the electron?

In Bohr's 1913 model the electron circles the proton on fixed orbits. It explained the colours of hydrogen's light, but the picture is wrong. Quantum mechanics (Schrödinger, 1926) replaces the orbit with a wave function ψ. Its square, |ψ|², gives the probability of finding the electron at each point.

What you see

Simple shape swaps the dots for the smooth lobes drawn in textbooks. It shows only the directions the electron prefers, not how far out it is.

Each dot is one place where the electron could be found if you looked. Where dots are dense, the electron is likely to be. The cloud does not move: these are stationary states. Colour shows the sign of the wave, positive or negative. Where the sign flips the probability is zero: a node. Use Cut open to see the nodes inside.

Three numbers

n, the shell, sets the energy, E = −13.6 eV / n², and the size, which grows as n².

l, the type (s, p, d, f for 0, 1, 2, 3), sets the angular momentum and the number of nodal planes or cones. It can be 0 to n − 1.

m, the shape, sets the orientation. There are 2l + 1 choices.

Light

Changing shell releases or takes up a photon with exactly the energy difference. Jumps ending on n = 2 give the visible Balmer lines (656 nm red, 486 nm blue-green); those ending on n = 1 are ultraviolet (Lyman), on n = 3 infrared (Paschen).

Bohr was partly right

The Bohr orbit has radius n² × 0.053 nm. Switch it on and compare: for the states with the largest l in each shell (1s, 2p, 3d, 4f) the cloud is densest at that distance.

About the model

The dots are drawn from the exact hydrogen wave functions, in the real-valued form used in chemistry (px, dxy and so on). Spin and relativistic corrections are left out. A real single-photon jump also needs l to change by 1; the diagram shows the energy of any change of shell. Infrared and ultraviolet photons are drawn dark red and violet.
