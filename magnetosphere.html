What happens to a charged particle

A magnetic field pushes a moving charge sideways, never forwards or backwards (the Lorentz force, F = q v × B). The particle keeps its speed but cannot travel in a straight line. Near Earth this gives three motions on top of each other.

1 · Gyration

The particle circles around a field line. Electrons and protons turn in opposite directions, and the circle gets tighter where the field is stronger.

2 · Bounce

Sliding along the line towards a pole, the particle meets a stronger field. Its motion along the line slows, stops at the mirror point and reverses. It bounces between the two hemispheres.

3 · Drift

The field is weaker farther out, so each circle is slightly lopsided and the particle creeps sideways: protons westward, electrons eastward. Together they form a ring of current around the planet.

Pitch angle and the aurora

The pitch angle is the angle between the velocity and the field. A large angle mirrors high above the ground. A small one (inside the loss cone) reaches the atmosphere before it can mirror, hits oxygen and nitrogen and makes them glow: green from oxygen at 100–250 km, red from oxygen higher up, violet from nitrogen at the lower edge.

About the model

The field is a tilted dipole, 31 000 nT at the equator. Orbits are integrated step by step from the Lorentz force alone, so mirroring and drift are not scripted. To make the motion visible, the circles are far larger than in reality and the aurora is drawn taller. The real field is also squeezed by the solar wind on the day side and stretched into a long tail at night, where most auroral particles are accelerated.
